Communication ​

Observing, in quantum mechanics, (probably) doesn’t mean what you think it does ​

Let’s try to clear up a misunderstanding I have often heard about quantum mechanics.

In a very famous Italian podcast, hosted by a very famous Italian rapper, I heard for the umpteenth time one thing: the idea that nature behaves differently if there is someone observing it. This was brought up by the very famous rapper/host, who (rightly!) is not very well versed in quantum mechanics. What is disappointing is that two guests, in a position to clarify this, did not seize the opportunity to do so. So we’ll do it here. I would like to convince you that:

  • Making an observation does not necessarily mean that there is someone observing the system; it has another meaning and brings with it other problems.
  • The fact that nature behaves differently “when observed” is fairly obvious.
  • There is a point on which classical physics is fundamentally different from quantum physics, which from some points of view is even more disturbing.

The measurement problem ​

Let’s start from the beginning: there is a physics called classical, it is the one you studied at school, and it is a theory that describes things on the scale of the events of our everyday life. There is a physics called quantum which is instead the physics of very small things: this is, for example, the physical theory that describes the interactions between things on the atomic scale.

The fundamental problem is that these two theories work in different ways and are based on different theoretical assumptions, and this represents an issue when one wants to make an observation at the microscopic level. The only way we have to observe a microscopic phenomenon is to build an apparatus, that is: something macroscopic (hence an object described by classical mechanics) that gives responses, for example by moving a pointer on a dial, so as to be able to distinguish different microscopic interactions (hence described by quantum mechanics). This is the problem of the observation: not the fact that there is a physicist in the room watching this thing happen, but the fact that we are putting in communication two phenomena that we describe in fundamentally different ways.

This makes the question interpretable in a very concrete way: a species of primates has invented rules that allow us to predict the outcome of certain phenomena on a macroscopic scale; the same species of primates has invented fundamentally different rules that allow us to predict the outcome of certain phenomena on a microscopic scale: when the two sets of rules come to blows, the primate is confused. That’s understandable, and it has nothing to do (not necessarily, at least; I’ll come back to it at the end) with a special role of the individuals of our species as observers of reality. It is not at all a new problem; read what John Stewart Bell, an important Northern Irish physicist, wrote in an article from his book of 1984.

But first let me argue against a myth... that quantum theory had undone somehow the Copernican revolution. From those who made that revolution we learned that the world is more intelligible when we do not imagine ourselves to be at the centre of it. Does not quantum theory again place 'observers'...us...at the centre of the picture? There is indeed much talk of 'observables' in quantum theory books. [...] The only 'observer' which is essential in orthodox practical quantum theory is the inanimate apparatus which amplifies microscopic events to macroscopic consequences.

So? Is there a solution? There exist several conceptual approaches to this problem, but the important thing to take home is that this does not undermine our ability to describe the outcomes of experiments and to predict the result of new experiments, which is the job of a scientific theory. In this regard, let me quote a very effective analogy from the same article.

It is as if our friends could not find words to tell us about the very strange places where they went on holiday. We could see for ourselves whether they came back browner or fatter. This would be enough for us to be able to advise other friends, who might wish to be browner or fatter, about those strange places. Our apparatus visits the microscopic world for us, and we see what happens to it as a result.

An aside, perhaps tangential ​

What almost makes one smile is that what is reported as the “absurd” thing about quantum mechanics is often expressed in a way so generic that it turns out to be true even for classical mechanics. That is, it is said that “nature behaves differently when observed”, but there are examples in which this happens also at the macroscopic level, hence regarding phenomena describable with classical physics; two examples:

  • You want to measure the temperature of a basin of water; to do this, you take a thermometer, that is, an object at a different temperature, and you put it in the basin: you have just changed the temperature of the water whose temperature you want to measure. The only way to avoid this is to bring the thermometer to the same temperature as the water before immersing it; it goes without saying that this implies already knowing the temperature of the water, making the measurement useless.
  • You want to measure the potential difference between two points of a circuit, so you take the terminals of the voltmeter and attach them to the two points on which you want to make the measurement: you have just created a circuit different from the one on which you wanted to make the measurement.

This matter of modifying what one wants to observe is a fairly ubiquitous circumstance (at least conceptually, even if it produces negligible effects) in physics, but I would say in science in general.

Is that all?? ​

Actually, no. I have neglected to tell you about the elephant in the room, which is one of the fundamental differences between the descriptions of reality of classical and quantum physics: I’ll do it now with an example. Disclaimer: as far as I know, an experiment like this has never been done and it would make no sense to do it, for many reasons on which it makes no sense for me to dwell now; it only serves to make a conceptual difference clear.

Imagine having a table, on which many little balls are arranged; an object is brought close to one side of the table, and we know that this object is positively charged. At this point, some balls move towards the side of the table where the object is, others instead go in the opposite direction.

Description of the phenomenon according to a classical paradigm: the balls have always been charged, some positively and others negatively, but we did not know it; bringing the object close made the charge of the particles known to us. The ignorance regarding the charge of the balls is epistemic, that is, it concerns the fact that we have not had experience of it.

Description of the phenomenon according to a quantum paradigm: the balls do not really have a well-defined charge until the object has been brought close, at which point they are, in a certain sense, “forced to decide” whether they are positive or negative and behave accordingly. The ignorance regarding the charge is ontological, that is, the charges did not have a well-defined charge before the interaction.

These interpretative paradigms are completely equivalent in this example. Both give rise to the same effective description, both work. If the first appears more sensible to us, it is by virtue of convictions deriving from our everyday experience, in which the physical properties of objects have a well-defined value. What one understands instead by studying quantum mechanics is that, if one wants to build a physical theory that also holds together other phenomena that appear at the microscopic level, one must abandon the first paradigm and embrace the second.

This is a change of paradigm, in the way measurements are understood, which is something far more destabilizing than the fact that “things behave differently if you look at them”. I am forced to stop here, the subject is long; if you are interested, sooner or later I will create on this site a space in which to explain this and other things properly, but today is not that day.

Is there a need to be so pedantic? ​

Perhaps so; it’s worth repeating: quantum mechanics is an extremely successful physical theory, which works perfectly in describing what it is supposed to describe. This means, in a nutshell, that it is more reliable than more or less anything you rely on every day in your life.

Surrounding it with a mystical aura, in my view, lends itself to an instrumental use of it to justify more or less anything; see for example the fact that attempts have been made to justify the efficacy of homeopathy by supposing the existence of a phenomenon called water memory, which in turn claimed to be justified by virtue of considerations linked to quantum mechanics. The short summary is that it is all bullshit.

I return to one thing, as I had promised you: if you believe that a god covers reality with a veil as soon as we human beings stop observing it, you could quite well be right, and we do not have the elements to refute it. Just be aware that quantum mechanics absolutely does not need such a view in order to work and does not represent a proof in favor of this view. In general, any scientific theory worthy of the name can neither refute nor confirm this view. They do two different jobs and speak to each other little.
